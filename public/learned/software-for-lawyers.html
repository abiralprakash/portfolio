Designing software for lawyers, not lawyers for software

LegalTech · Domain modeling

Generic legal software often imports CRM vocabulary — leads, pipelines, opportunities — and asks firms to reorganize decades of habit around it. IP practice in Nepal already has a physical grammar: a client company, its file cabinet, matters opened for specific filings, assets such as trademarks and patents hanging off those matters. When NepalIPMS mapped Company → File → Matter → Asset, partners recognized their world on screen. When we used abstract “deals,” they did not.

Language is part of the architecture. Practitioners say “renewal window,” “opposition,” “DoI status,” not “task due date” or “stage changed.” Labels in the UI are not marketing copy; they are trust signals. A wrong word suggests the builder never sat through a filing week.

Workflow beats feature count. The win was not adding another dashboard widget — it was making the path from bulletin import to updated matter record short enough that staff would choose the system over a spreadsheet. Every extra modal on a daily path is a reason to revert to paper.

Permissions mirror partnership structure. Juniors see matters they work on; partners see portfolios; admins configure firm-wide register imports. Legal software fails when it is either too open with client data or so locked down that people share passwords.

Confidentiality shapes technical choices too: audit trails on document access, export controls, and clear separation between firm matters and national register data that is public but still sensitive in aggregate. Lawyers evaluate risk for a living. The product must feel careful.

The deepest lesson: respect the desk. Software wins when it disappears into how lawyers already think — not when it wins design awards for looking unlike anything they have used before.
