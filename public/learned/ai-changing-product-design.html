How AI is changing the way I design products

Applied AI · Product craft

Two years ago my design process ended at “what the user clicks.” Now it also asks “what the system should read, retrieve, and refuse to invent.” AI did not replace research or UX — it added a layer where context quality and failure modes are as visible as button placement. For legal and education products, that shift is not optional.

I stopped starting with chat bubbles. The first question is whether generation is needed at all. Often the win is search that understands synonyms, OCR that pre-fills a form, or a timeline assembled from existing matter events. When generation helps — drafting a client update from matter notes, summarizing a long bulletin entry — it sits on top of retrieval, with sources attached so a lawyer can verify every sentence.

Guardrails are UX. Scope limits (“this assistant only knows this matter”), explicit “I don’t know” states, and blocked actions on filing or sending without confirmation are not backend details — they are how users sleep at night. A confident wrong answer in consumer software is annoying. In LegalTech it is malpractice-shaped.

Latency and cost changed how I prototype. Running retrieval and small models at the edge via Workers AI makes experiments shippable without a GPU budget. But I still design for fallback: if the model is down, search and forms must work. AI features should degrade gracefully, not take the desk hostage.

User research looks different too. I watch where experts edit model output — those edits are the roadmap. The product is learning when paralegals consistently rewrite the same paragraph, or when counsellors ignore suggested text entirely. Iteration is prompt and pipeline tuning as much as pixel polish.

AI did not make design easier. It made honest product thinking more important. The interface is still where trust is won or lost — especially when the machine is involved.
